Module 01 规模

Why China, and why care

Before any tactics: what the market actually is, how big your realistic slice could be, and the single most common strategic mistake European SMEs make — treating China as one market.

1.1

Think in city tiers, not in billions

›

The "1.4 billion consumers" pitch is a trap. Purchasing power, tastes and channels differ enormously between Tier-1 cities (Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen — mature, brand-saturated, expensive to enter), Tier-2 (Chengdu, Hangzhou, Wuhan and ~30 others — fast-growing, more open to new brands) and lower tiers, where price sensitivity dominates.

  • Successful SMEs pick a beachhead: one segment, in one or two cities, through one channel.
  • A single Tier-2 city can have more inhabitants than Austria. "Small" in China is still large.
  • Distance matters: Chengdu to Shanghai is roughly Lisbon to Warsaw.
Ask yourself "which 5 million people?" instead of "how do I reach 1.4 billion?". The first question has an answer.
1.2

What Europe is already good at selling

›

EU exporters do best where provenance and trust carry a premium: machinery and precision equipment, cars and components, pharmaceuticals, medical devices, food and drink (dairy, wine, meat, olive oil), cosmetics, and design or heritage brands. Food-safety scandals made "imported from Europe" a quality signal that Chinese consumers still pay for.

Two caveats: local competitors are excellent and getting better — the guochao ("national wave") trend makes home-grown brands fashionable — and a premium position must be earned with storytelling, certification and channel choice, not assumed.

1.3

The consumer is mobile-first and research-heavy

›

Chinese consumers discover products on Xiaohongshu and Douyin, verify them through reviews and key opinion leaders, buy inside Tmall, JD or a livestream, and pay with a phone. Word-of-mouth is industrialised: a single credible review thread can matter more than a year of advertising.

For you this means the buying journey is researchable — you can watch demand for your category before spending a euro on entry. Module 05 shows how.

Module 02 入市

Choosing your entry route

There are five realistic doors into China. They differ in cost, control, speed and how hard they are to exit. Most first-timers should start with the cheapest reversible option that tests real demand.

2.1

Exporting through a distributor or agent

›

Fastest and cheapest: a Chinese partner imports, clears customs and sells through their channels. You trade control and margin for their licences, relationships and warehouse.

  • Vet the partner hard (Module 03) and never grant nationwide exclusivity to an untested distributor — start with one region or channel, with performance targets.
  • Register your trademark (including a Chinese name) before the first conversation.
2.2

Cross-border e-commerce (CBEC) — the test lab

›

CBEC lets you sell consumer goods to Chinese shoppers without a Chinese legal entity, through platforms like Tmall Global or JD Worldwide. Orders ship from bonded warehouses in China or directly from Europe, under a simplified tax regime, for products on the government's CBEC "positive list".

  • Ideal for testing demand for food, cosmetics, mother-and-baby, supplements and lifestyle brands.
  • Some registrations required for general trade (e.g. certain NMPA filings) are simplified under CBEC — a major shortcut for cosmetics and supplements.
  • Budget honestly: platform deposits, commissions and marketing mean "cheap to enter" is not "free to win".
Many EU brands run 12–18 months of CBEC as market research they get paid for, then decide whether to invest onshore.
2.3

WFOE, joint venture, licensing

›

A WFOE (wholly foreign-owned enterprise) is your own Chinese company: full control, ability to hire, invoice in RMB and hold licences — at the price of months of setup, registered capital and ongoing accounting. A joint venture shares ownership with a local partner: sometimes required or genuinely useful for channels, always dependent on partner quality. Licensing or franchising is asset-light but puts your IP in someone else's hands — only with registered IP and a strong contract.

  • Rule of thumb: don't build a WFOE to find out whether demand exists. Build one after something cheaper proved it.

Module 03 规则

Rules, paperwork and protecting yourself

Nothing here is exotic — it's just different, and unforgiving of assumptions. Five areas cover most of the pain European SMEs report: trademarks, product compliance, contracts, data, and partner due diligence.

3.1

IP: China is first-to-file. Act accordingly.

›

Trademark rights go to whoever registers first — prior use abroad counts for little. "Trademark squatting" (registering a foreign brand to sell it back or block it) is a professional industry.

  • File with CNIPA before any exposure: trade fairs, meetings, samples, even a hopeful LinkedIn post.
  • Register the Chinese-character name too — the market will invent one for you if you don't, and someone else will register it.
  • Your EU trademark does not protect you in China. Budget a few thousand euros; compare that with buying your own name back.
The China IP SME Helpdesk (EU-funded) gives free confidential advice to European SMEs. Use it before your first fair.
3.2

Product compliance: CCC, GB standards, labels

›

Many product categories (electronics, toys, vehicle parts…) need the CCC mark (China Compulsory Certification) before they can be sold. Technical requirements live in GB standards — China's equivalent of EN standards, sometimes aligned, sometimes not. Consumer goods need compliant Chinese-language labels; food exporters need facility registration with customs (GACC); cosmetics go through NMPA notification or registration.

  • Check whether your product needs CCC first — it drives cost and timeline.
  • CBEC (Module 02) softens some requirements, which is precisely why it's the favourite test channel.
3.3

Contracts and the almighty chop

›

In China, companies are bound by their official company chop (seal) more than by signatures. A contract signed by a "general manager" but never chopped may be worthless; a chopped contract binds even if the signer lacked authority.

  • Contract with the registered Chinese legal name (in characters), not the English trading name.
  • Use a bilingual contract and be explicit about which language governs — in practice, Chinese courts work with the Chinese text.
  • Agree dispute resolution up front; CIETAC arbitration is a common, enforceable choice.
3.4

Data and due diligence

›

PIPL — China's personal-information law, in spirit a cousin of the GDPR — plus data-security rules restrict how personal and "important" data crosses the border. If you'll run e-commerce, CRM or an app touching Chinese users, plan for it.

For any partner: get their Unified Social Credit Code, pull the official company registry record, check registered capital, scope of business and litigation history, and visit (or commission an audit of) the factory. An honest partner expects this; resistance is information.

Module 04 关系

Business culture decoded

Deals in China run on relationships built before and around the contract. You don't need to perform Chineseness — sincerity travels — but you do need to avoid a handful of unforced errors.

4.1

Guanxi: the relationship is the infrastructure

›

Guanxi (关系) is the web of reciprocal relationships that business moves through. A warm introduction outperforms a cold email by an order of magnitude; time spent on dinners, visits and small favours is not overhead — it is the work.

  • Expect several meetings before substance. Rushing signals unreliability, not efficiency.
  • Reciprocity is tracked. Accept help graciously and return it deliberately.
  • Guanxi is personal: if your China manager leaves, relationships may leave too. Institutionalise contacts across your team.
4.2

Mianzi: face is a hard currency

›

Mianzi (面子) — dignity and social standing — is gained, given and lost publicly. Causing someone to lose face (public correction, blunt refusal, visible impatience) can quietly end a deal that looked healthy.

  • Disagree in private; in the room, use "we will study this" language yourself.
  • A hesitant answer, a subject change, or "maybe / it's inconvenient" is very often a polite no. Pushing for a hard "no" forces a face-losing moment.
  • Give face generously: praise the counterpart's company to their boss, acknowledge seniority, remember titles.
4.3

Meetings, banquets and WeChat

›

Hierarchy is visible: the senior person enters first, sits centre-facing-the-door, and speaks for the side. Match seniority in your own delegation. Business cards are exchanged with two hands and read, not pocketed. The banquet is a working venue: seating follows rank, the host toasts first, and business often waits until the end — or the next morning.

  • WeChat replaces email for most day-to-day contact. Print a QR code on your card.
  • Silence in negotiation is a tool, not a problem to fill. Whoever fills it usually concedes something.
You will be forgiven almost any etiquette slip if you are visibly sincere, patient and prepared. These cards prevent the slips that are hard to forgive.

Module 05 数字

Digital China: a parallel internet

Google, Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and YouTube are blocked. Payments are phone-based. Shopping is entertainment. Everything you know about digital marketing has a Chinese counterpart — rarely the same one.

5.1

Your website barely exists there

›

Behind the Great Firewall, sites hosted in Europe load slowly or not at all — especially if they embed Google fonts, maps, analytics or YouTube video (they hang the whole page). Search happens on Baidu, not Google.

  • Quick wins: strip blocked third-party embeds from your site, or build a lightweight China-facing page on a Hong Kong / China-optimised CDN.
  • Hosting inside mainland China requires an ICP filing — which requires a Chinese entity. Most SMEs don't need this on day one.
  • Reality check: your brand's true "website" in China will be a store page on Tmall/JD and an official account on WeChat or Xiaohongshu.
5.2

Super-apps and mobile money

›

WeChat is messaging, payments, ID, mini-program app store and CRM in one; Alipay is the other wallet. Mobile payment is near-universal in cities — cash is rare and foreign cards often aren't accepted at all.

  • For business travel: both apps now accept foreign cards — set them up before you fly.
  • For selling: a WeChat Official Account + mini-program can act as your store, loyalty scheme and newsletter combined.
5.3

Commerce is content: KOLs and livestreams

›

Shopping is discovery-led: Xiaohongshu (RED) for trusted lifestyle reviews, Douyin (TikTok's Chinese sibling) for short video and live commerce, where top hosts sell more in an evening than a department store does in a year. Key opinion leaders (KOLs) and smaller KOCs (consumers) are a standard, priced media channel with agencies and rate cards.

  • Calendar matters: 11.11 (Singles' Day) and 6.18 festivals concentrate huge shares of annual sales.
  • Start small: seeding products with 20 micro-KOCs on Xiaohongshu is a classic, affordable first experiment.

Module 06 资金

Money, logistics and getting paid

Deals die in the plumbing: payment terms nobody priced, Incoterms nobody read, margins nobody landed. This module is the plumbing.

6.1

The currency and the controls

›

China's currency is the renminbi (RMB / CNY), and it is not freely convertible: cross-border flows pass through SAFE (the foreign-exchange administration) rules and bank paperwork. Money moves — millions of transactions prove it daily — but it moves on documentation, not on goodwill.

  • Fix the invoice currency (EUR, USD or RMB) and who carries FX risk in the contract.
  • Repatriating profits from a WFOE is routine but paperwork-heavy: audited accounts, taxes settled, then dividends.
  • Expect your Chinese customer's bank to want contracts and invoices that match the payment exactly.
6.2

Incoterms: three letters that move thousands of euros

›

Incoterms define where your cost and risk end. For a first-time exporter the practical range is:

  • EXW (Ex Works): buyer collects at your door — simplest for you, least attractive to them.
  • FOB (Free On Board): you deliver cleared onto the ship; buyer pays main freight and carries onward risk. The workhorse.
  • CIF: you also pay freight and minimum insurance to the Chinese port — more service, more of your working capital.
  • DDP (Delivered Duty Paid): you handle everything including Chinese import duty and VAT. Avoid until you know exactly what you're doing.

Landed cost stack on the China side: duty + import VAT (13% standard) + possibly consumption tax + port charges + distributor and platform margins. Price backwards from the Chinese shelf, not forwards from your EU price list.

6.3

Sea, rail or air

›
  • Sea: ~30–45 days Europe–China, cheapest per kilo, king for full containers.
  • Rail (China–Europe Railway Express): ~15–25 days, mid-priced — a genuine option for time-sensitive mid-value goods.
  • Air: 3–7 days, expensive — samples, launches, perishables.
CBEC parcels ride a different lane: bonded-warehouse stock in China clears in hours, which is how "imported" goods arrive next-day.
